On 26 July the Prime Minister announced a six-member task force under Nandan Nilekani to make India's examinations, in his words, reliable and transparent. The day before, the Education Minister had resigned. Five weeks before that, some 20 lakh candidates had sat NEET-UG for the second time in seven weeks, because the first paper had leaked.

It is worth being precise about how it leaked, because the correct version undercuts the reform that followed. The early story was a printing press. What the CBI told the court on 15 May was that the leak traced back to a source inside the National Testing Agency — and the arrest announced was of a chemistry lecturer working with the NTA who had access to the papers and dictated questions and answers at his residence before the exam.

Not a press. A person with access.

Every reform proposed in that week — the task force, the amended penal law, the promised move to computer-based testing in 2027 — addresses the security of the question paper. I want to argue that this is the wrong object, and that we can now say so with documents rather than suspicion. In the last two years the Enforcement Directorate, the Central Board of Direct Taxes, the Central Bureau of Investigation, several High Courts and the Supreme Court have each put on the record a piece of a system that has almost nothing to do with question papers.

A medical seat in India is worth crores. Everything else is a market that has formed around that price.

There are three such markets. One sells information before the exam — the leak economy. One sells allocation after it — the seat market, which is where the real money is and which no examination reform touches. One sells preparation around it — the coaching industry. They have different customers and different mechanics, but they are priced off the same shortage, and closing any one re-prices the other two.

India keeps regulating the markets. Nobody is regulating the scarcity.

And there is a sharper failure underneath. In the week the task force was announced, a Bill was moved doubling sentences for paper leaks from three-to-five years to five-to-ten, and raising organised-crime fines from ₹1 crore to ₹10 crore. Every lever pulled was severity. Meanwhile the Central Vigilance Commission's most recent report shows 7,072 CBI-investigated corruption cases pending trial at the end of 2024, 2,660 of them for more than ten years, 379 for more than twenty.

The criminological evidence here is not ambiguous. The United States National Institute of Justice states it plainly: it is the certainty of being caught that deters, not the severity of the punishment, and increases in already-lengthy sentences produce at best a very modest deterrent effect. India has been multiplying the term that barely matters while the terms that do sit near zero.

How to read this. Each entry separates what is documented — quoted from an official record I have read — from what is reported but not independently locatable, and from what is not established however widely repeated. I have been strict about those lines, and in one case strictness cost me my best example. The most damaging thing about this subject is not that people exaggerate it. It is that the true version is stranger than the popular one, and gets crowded out by it.

AThree marketsOne shortage, priced three ways

01
Well coveredSmallest market

The market in information

Mechanism

A guess paper circulating in coaching hubs was found to match the live NEET-UG paper on over a hundred questions. The exam was cancelled on 12 May; the CBI took over; a re-test ran on 21 June under Air Force transport and paper-setter lockdown. Of roughly 22.7 lakh registered candidates, over 2.5 lakh did not return for it.

This is the market everyone can see, because a leak leaves an artefact — someone can hold up a file and count the matching questions. That visibility is exactly why it absorbs the reform attention, and why the other two markets have been left alone.

But note what the investigation actually found. The compromise was not a failure of paper logistics. It was a person inside the agency with access to the content. Which means the reform on offer — moving the paper onto screens — addresses a vulnerability that was not the one exploited.

What to build
  1. Move to computer-based testing, and stop selling it as a solution. It relocates the attack surface from one press to a thousand local networks. JEE Main Session 4 was compromised in 2021 through remote-access software on exam terminals with centre-supervisor collusion. The controls were not defeated in software; they were walked around by the people running the room.
  2. Design against the insider, because the insider is who did it. Split-key custody of the item bank across holders in different institutions, compartmentalised authorship so no one person sees both the bank and the live form, and immutable access logs held outside the agency.
  3. Fund a standing data-forensics capability — response-time anomalies, answer-similarity indices, score-to-record discordance — and publish what it finds. Distributed digital fraud leaves no artefact. If no anomaly is ever reported, the detection system is broken, not the system.

TestThe first published integrity report names a non-zero number and the method that found it.

02
Largest marketOfficially documented

The market in allocation

Mechanism

A seat that a candidate declines does not vanish. It falls into a later counselling round, and in the private and deemed-university system it can be filled at a far higher fee with a cash premium on top. So the profitable move is not to steal a question paper. It is to ensure a seat becomes vacant late enough to be sold.

In the Telangana postgraduate case the Enforcement Directorate provisionally attached ₹5.34 crore in November 2024 — ₹3.33 crore from one college, ₹2.01 crore from another — bringing the cumulative value of assets seized, frozen and attached across that investigation to ₹9.71 crore. Officers estimated a cash trail of around ₹100 crore between 2016 and 2022.

The larger number is older. After searches across nine trusts and fifty-six premises in Karnataka and Kerala in February 2021, the Central Board of Direct Taxes reported detection of ₹402.78 crore of capitation, with ₹15.09 crore in cash and 81 kilograms of gold seized.

Set that against the entrance-exam story. The leak economy sells a rank. This one sells the seat, and it has been doing so, on the record, for a decade.

Documented — quoted from records I have read

  • ED provisional attachment order, 29 November 2024 (Telangana); CBDT press release, February 2021 (Karnataka and Kerala)
  • Allahabad High Court finding, recorded verbatim by the Supreme Court in Bhavna Tiwari: "80% of the seats were filled up in the mop up round"

Reported but not independently locatable

  • The Medical Counselling Committee's identification of "about a thousand candidates" engaged in the practice, attributed to a 2018 Supreme Court order I could not retrieve in full text
  • A 2019 Telangana High Court finding that 111 convenor-quota postgraduate seats went untaken while no private college seat fell vacant. The judgment exists; the figure I could not confirm

Not established

  • Any arrest, chargesheet or prosecution complaint in the Telangana case. The public record stops at the attachment order
  • Any criminal charge against a candidate for seat blocking anywhere in India — sanctions have been administrative only
  • Any FIR arising from the ₹402.78 crore CBDT finding
What to build
  1. A single-run, binding match. India's multi-round counselling is the vulnerability: every mop-up round is a fresh opportunity to free and resell a seat. A deferred-acceptance clearing — the family of algorithms for which Roth and Shapley shared the 2012 Nobel Memorial Prize for the theory of stable allocations and the practice of market design, and whose Roth–Peranson variant has run the American residency match since 1998 — removes the window rather than policing it.
  2. Design it against de-reservation from the first line of the specification. There is no single national merit list in India; there are category and quota-wise lists. A clearing rule that ignores that would quietly convert unfilled reserved-category seats into open ones. Any post-match vacancy must go to the next eligible candidate on the merit list for that seat's category, at government fee, with the college holding no discretion over who.
  3. Make the vacancy worthless instead of preventing it. A manufactured vacancy that yields the college nothing stops being manufactured.
  4. Sanction the institution, not the instrument. The American match bars programmes, not only applicants — one to three years or permanently — and publishes them where their accreditor can see. In India the penalty must be seat forfeiture to the government pool. A fine is a line item against a seat worth crores.

TestPost-match vacancies are allotted centrally, category-wise, at government fee — and the count of seats filled outside the match is published each cycle.

03
Least evidenced hereCultural

The market in preparation

Mechanism

The third market sells the attempt itself. It is the one I can document least well and the one that shapes the other two most, because it manufactures the demand they price against — and because the pressure to produce visible rankers is what pulls some coaching operations toward buying papers rather than teaching.

Why the funnel is so narrow is partly a question of information rather than economics. Families choose a path with a low single-digit success rate because those odds are known; unconventional paths have odds nobody can state. Ambiguity aversion is a well-described bias, and here it operates at national scale. What families are buying is not really a probability. It is the belief that effort maps onto outcome.

Not established

  • I have no audited figure for the size of the coaching industry, and I am not going to repeat one. The widely circulated numbers do not survive sourcing
  • The claim that coaching centres purchase papers is credible and widely reported, but I have not verified it against a named prosecution in this cycle
What to build
  1. Publish the odds honestly, disaggregated. Selection rate by district, by board, by school type, by category, by gender, by disability status and by medium of instruction, alongside the real cost of a two-year attempt. A national success rate tells a family in a Telugu-medium government school nothing about their own odds, which are not the national figure.
  2. Widen the legitimate funnel. Nursing, allied health, public health and clinical research need to become paths a family can name and respect. That is a labour-market and pay-scale question, not a counselling-poster question.

BWhat the record showsIncluding where the popular account is wrong

04
CorrectionLoad-bearing

The scam is real. The story about it is wrong.

The popular version

Middlemen pay top-ranking candidates to enter counselling and block seats they never intend to take, then have them withdraw late so the college can resell the freed seat for crores. The topper is a paid accomplice.

What the file says

Close to the opposite. The Enforcement Directorate's attachment order states that private medical colleges, in collusion with consultants and middlemen, were "engaged in seat blocking using the certificates/documents of high-ranking students," that the blocked seats were retained until the mop-up round, and — the detail that reframes everything — that "such penalty was arranged for by the private medical colleges themselves."

It also records that "some candidates claimed they had not applied for registration under the Management Quota at KNRUHS." Around forty-five seats were flagged as blocked using the names of students from northern states.

So the high-ranking student whose rank makes the block valuable is, in the pattern the ED describes, not the profiteer. Their documents are the instrument. And the anti-blocking penalty was, on the ED's own finding, arranged by the colleges — which makes it a transaction cost, not a deterrent.

Documented

  • ED attachment order, 29 November 2024, quoted above — including the finding that colleges funded the exit penalties

Reported but not independently locatable

  • A 2019 Telangana High Court observation that candidates were "used as pawns" — corroborated in contemporaneous reporting, though I could not retrieve the judgment text
  • A 2022 Telangana High Court finding that the ₹3 lakh anti-blocking penalty was paid by the colleges and that students' bonds were held in college custody; and a 2024 Karnataka High Court order imposing ₹15 lakh in exemplary costs and surrendering ten seats to the state merit quota. Both are cited widely; neither judgment text could I locate. I have not built the argument on them
  • An account, attributed to investigation documents, that middlemen source names from published candidate lists and obtain one-time passwords to register on candidates' behalf. This is not in the ED release and I could not source it. It is the reason I have dropped the recommendation it would have supported

Not established

  • No FIR, chargesheet, ED release, tax department release or judgment anywhere in India documents an agent paying a topper to enter counselling in order to block a seat. The claim traces to unsourced journalism from 2019, and the tax department's own release covering the same facts declines to repeat it
Why the correction matters

If the topper is a paid accomplice, the policy response is to punish candidates — which is exactly what has happened. Uttar Pradesh debarred 148 candidates in September 2025. Individual candidates have faced lakh-rupee forfeitures and multi-year debarments, and at least one such forfeiture was struck down by a High Court as confiscation without authority of law.

If the student's documents are the instrument, that entire enforcement posture is aimed at the wrong end of the transaction — and in some cases at people who did not know a transaction had occurred in their name.

That correction is not mine. It was won in court, largely by candidates themselves, often without institutional backing: Shivani Singh in Allahabad, Akula Harshini in Telangana, Bhavna Tiwari all the way to the Supreme Court, Remika Devi in Delhi. Every reframing in this section rests on a young person who was penalised, refused to accept it, and litigated. They appear below as case names; they are the reason there is a record to cite at all. The doctors who publicly demanded arrests in December 2024 are the other half of that record.

What to build
  1. Reopen the debarments. This one is not prospective. The 148 candidates debarred in Uttar Pradesh, and every penalty imposed on a candidate for a block they did not arrange, reviewed against the ED's own finding that the colleges funded the penalties. Where a candidate never applied, the debarment is vacated, the forfeiture refunded with interest, and the lost attempt restored. A system that discovers it punished the wrong people and then only fixes itself going forward has not corrected anything.
  2. Bind every counselling action to the candidate, with a fallback that never excludes them. Registration, choice-filling, allotment and exit each confirmed to the candidate directly and acknowledged by them. If a student can be allotted a seat without ever knowing, the system has no idea who its user is. But see the guardrail in section E: identity binding must never mean that an authentication failure ends someone's year.
  3. Follow the penalty payment. The most incriminating fact on the record is that colleges paid students' exit penalties. That is a traceable financial transaction identifying the beneficiary directly. Make it the trigger for institutional sanction rather than an incidental finding.
  4. Stop letting colleges hold original certificates. Retention is defended as an anti-blocking measure. It is also the leverage that makes a student unable to extract themselves from an institution — and for a woman whose family is already deciding whether she may study away from home, an institution holding her certificates compounds coercion she is already under.

TestThe next sanction in a seat-blocking case falls on an institution, and a candidate registered without their knowledge is treated as a complainant rather than an offender.

05
Natural experimentCuts both ways

What verification did to one quota, and not to another

The finding

The NRI quota is filled by candidates claiming to be the ward of a non-resident sponsor. The fraud is straightforward: manufacture the relationship. Attaching ₹12.33 crore in a West Bengal and Odisha case in May 2025, the Enforcement Directorate found that agents "had also approached and obtained credentials of unrelated NRIs by paying money" — and that colleges paid those agents ₹1 to ₹1.5 crore per MBBS seat and ₹3 to ₹4 crore per postgraduate seat.

Then verification arrived in the undergraduate stream. With embassy attestation of the sponsor relationship required, the number of candidates converting to the NRI category in NEET-UG counselling fell from 1,846 in 2024 to 188 in 2025. A ninety per cent collapse from one procedural step.

And then the control group

I wanted this to be the clean proof of the argument. It is not, and the reason is more useful than the proof would have been.

In the postgraduate stream, run by the same committee in the same year, 1,666 candidates converted to the NRI category in 2025 against 1,904 the year before — a decline of about twelve per cent, not ninety. Of the 811 who converted in the third round, a reported 698 qualified by citing relatives as sponsors.

Same regulator, same year, two streams, two completely different outcomes. What differed was whether the relationship actually had to be proved to a third party, or could be asserted through a relative.

That is the thesis in one natural experiment. Certainty works — and only where it is actually applied. Severity was identical in both streams and did nothing in either.

The sequel

In September 2024 the Punjab and Haryana High Court struck down a definition of "NRI ward" extending eligibility to uncles, aunts, grandparents and cousins; the Supreme Court declined to interfere. A counselling notice of July 2025 expressly excluded such relatives as bona fide guardians. A further notice in May 2026 lists them as eligible sponsors again.

There is, as far as I can establish, no National Medical Commission regulation defining "NRI ward" at all. A category worth crores per seat is governed by whichever notice was issued most recently.

Not established

  • The widely repeated figure of "18,000 fraudulent NRI admissions" does not survive checking. It appears to derive from a report noting that NRI seats account for roughly 15% of India's MBBS seats — 15% of 1.2 lakh. It is the size of the quota, not a count of fraud
  • The Chief Justice's remark that "this NRI business is nothing but a fraud" was made orally in September 2024 while declining to interfere. It is not a holding and should not be cited as one
  • Documented EWS-certificate fraud in medical admissions is far thinner than commonly asserted. The substantial forgery record is in school admissions
What to build
  1. Write the definition into regulation. A category worth crores per seat, governed by a counselling notice rather than a rule, will be redefined by whoever issues the next notice.
  2. Certify the commercial categories before the exam — and only those. NRI, management and institutional sponsorship verified at registration, months before anyone knows they would benefit, with a challenge window open to the regulator and the counselling authority. Fraud committed blind is more expensive and more detectable.
  3. Do not extend this to reservation, and do not make any challenge window public. SC, ST, OBC, EWS and disability certification is slow, discretionary and extractive at the tehsil and district-medical-board level, and costs the applicant travel and time they may not have. An embassy attestation is a service a wealthy family buys in a week; a caste or disability certificate is a queue a poor family stands in. Front-loading certification is progressive applied to a category that exists to sell seats and regressive applied to a category that exists to open them. And a public mechanism to contest another person's reservation status is not an integrity measure — it is a harassment instrument with a long history in Indian institutions. Contested reservation claims run through the issuing authority, never through the public.
  4. Publish the conversion counts every cycle, by stream. The 1,846-to-188 collapse and the 1,904-to-1,666 non-collapse were legible only because someone counted. Make it an annual statutory publication and the divergence between streams becomes impossible to ignore.

TestAn NMC regulation defines "NRI ward"; postgraduate conversions fall the way undergraduate conversions did.

06
Regulator captureUnder investigation

The watchdog that cannot see

Mechanism

A private medical college needs recognition to exist and more recognition to expand. Recognition depends on inspection. Inspection is episodic, scheduled, and conducted by people.

In June 2025 the CBI registered a case naming around 34 accused, among them officials of the health ministry and assessors of the National Medical Commission. The conduct alleged includes ghost faculty, patients arranged for the day, inspection schedules leaked in advance, and tampered biometric attendance. No chargesheet has been reported.

The scale of the underlying problem is not contested by the regulator. In an assessment of 246 colleges across 27 states, the Commission's own finding was that "none of the colleges met with the requirement of even 50% attendance" and that a majority had ghost faculty and senior residents or had not employed the required faculty at all.

Why the standard fix fails

The usual proposal — an autonomous, well-paid regulator — is the promise on which the National Medical Commission itself replaced the Medical Council of India. Pay does not prevent capture. Nor does technology on its own: biometric attendance has been defeated by fake fingerprints, retrospective tampering, and colleges blocking de-registration so a doctor stays on the rolls in two places at once. In July 2026 the Commission had to direct seventy colleges to link their CCTV to its central monitoring network.

What to build
  1. Continuous signal instead of inspection days. You can hire faculty for a week and rent equipment for an afternoon. You cannot fake a year of clinical activity. Regulate against encounters per student per month and supervision ratios — computed inside the hospital and reported as aggregate counts — and ghost capacity becomes an anomaly in a time series rather than something an inspector has to catch in the act. Patient-level records must never leave the institution; see section E.
  2. Regulate clinical capacity, not buildings. This is the deeper correction. The scarce resource in medical education was never the lecture hall — lectures are the replicable part. It is the supervised patient encounter. Measure expansion in supervised placements and the fake-infrastructure scam loses its object.
  3. Randomise the inspector, fix the evidence. Assessors drawn by lot from a national pool, never twice to the same state, submitting geotagged and timestamped media into a central system rather than a written report.
  4. Restore publication. Assessment reports, faculty by registration number and full-time-equivalent status, and clinical volumes back in the public domain. Students and journalists are a larger inspectorate than the Commission will ever fund, and they are already motivated.

TestAssessment reports are public again, and at least one recognition decision turns on continuous clinical-volume data rather than an inspection visit.

CThe wrong leverSeverity is rising while certainty falls

07
Central claimTestable

India pulled the lever that does not work

The pattern

The Public Examinations (Prevention of Unfair Means) Amendment Bill, 2026 raises the standard sentence from three-to-five years to five-to-ten; the general fine from ₹10 lakh to ₹50 lakh; the service-provider fine from ₹1 crore to ₹5 crore; the organised-crime fine to ₹10 crore; and service-provider debarment from four years to eight.

There was already a 2024 Act carrying three-to-five year sentences on the statute book when the 2026 paper leaked.

Against that, the certainty side: 7,072 CBI-investigated corruption cases pending trial at the end of 2024, 2,660 of them for more than a decade, 379 for more than twenty years. In the largest documented medical seat-blocking case in the country, nobody has been arrested. In the ₹402.78 crore capitation finding, no FIR has been traced. I could find exactly one instance of an institution being penalised for seat blocking, for ₹15 lakh — in a market where a single postgraduate seat has been valued at ₹1 to ₹2.5 crore.

The expected cost of corruption is the probability of being caught, times the probability of conviction, times the penalty, discounted by delay. India has been multiplying the third term while the first two approach zero and the discount approaches total.

What to build
  1. Legislate time limits, not sentences. The 2026 Bill's own fast-track provisions — investigation in two months, trial concluded within three months of chargesheet — are the parts worth defending. Extend that architecture to medical-admission corruption, where the pendency data says it is needed far more.
  2. Report certainty as a published statistic. Cases registered, chargesheeted, tried, and median time to disposal — laid before Parliament annually for examination and admission offences specifically. Right now the severity numbers are the only ones in the press release.
  3. Prefer administrative certainty to criminal severity. Seat forfeiture, recognition withdrawal and debarment can be applied in months on a civil standard. A ten-year sentence arriving in 2040, if it arrives, deters nobody in 2027.

TestMedian time from FIR to disposal in examination and admission cases is published, and falls.

08
DocumentaryUnreported

The deterrent got weaker after the Court strengthened it

The sequence

On 29 April 2025, in State of U.P. v. Bhavna Tiwari, the Supreme Court issued ten directions on medical counselling: a synchronised national calendar, fee disclosure before choices are made, centralised fee regulation, Aadhaar-based seat tracking, forfeiture and disqualification for repeat offenders, blacklisting of complicit colleges, third-party audits.

Now compare two documents. The postgraduate counselling bulletin for 2024 provided that a candidate who did not join after a stray-round allotment "shall be debarred from NEET PG examination for subsequent 1 (one) year along with forfeiture of security deposit." The bulletin for 2025, published after those directions, provides only "forfeiture of security deposit."

The one-year debarment was deleted. After the judgment.

Aadhaar-based seat tracking — the seventh direction — does not appear in either 2025 bulletin. Aadhaar features only as an acceptable form of photo identification.

And in March 2026 the Delhi High Court held, in a narrower but pointed ruling, that the authorities "cannot, by administrative interpretation, impose an additional penalty of ineligibility for participation in a SPMD Seats Counselling process." The specific sanction at issue there was found to have no basis in the rules as written.

What this means

  • The highest court in the country issued ten directions; the operative sanction was quietly removed from the counselling rules in the following cycle
  • The tracking mechanism the Court directed did not appear at all
  • I could find no Supreme Court compliance-monitoring order following those directions
What to build
  1. Court directions need a named compliance owner and a return date. Ten directions with no listed compliance hearing is a wish list. One direction with a date and an officer is enforcement.
  2. Never tie a live sanction to an unbuilt system. Write sanctions against instruments that are already running.
  3. Version-control the rules in public. A published, machine-readable diff of every counselling bulletin against the last. This deletion was findable only by reading two long PDFs side by side. It should have been a headline.
  4. And publish it in the languages the exam is written in. NEET is offered in thirteen. Every transparency measure in this piece — the bulletin diff, the integrity report, the certainty statistics, the conversion counts — is worthless to a candidate who cannot read it. A rule a Tamil- or Assamese-medium candidate cannot read is not a published rule.

TestThe next bulletins restore an enforceable sanction, in every examination language, and a compliance hearing is listed.

DThe rootWhat none of this touches

Everything above is enforcement design, and enforcement design has a ceiling. Corruption follows discretion, and discretion follows price.

Management-quota postgraduate seats in radiodiagnosis at private colleges in Rajasthan have carried published fees reported at ₹1.5 crore a year — ₹4.5 crore for the course. Among deemed universities, roughly four in ten postgraduate courses now exceed ₹1 crore for the course, with the highest radiodiagnosis fee around ₹76 lakh a year. Government postgraduate fees are lower by two to three orders of magnitude, depending on the institution.

The legal price has absorbed what used to be the illegal one. You can automate the entire allocation pipeline and, so long as a seat carries that much rent, somebody will find the discretion that remains.

This is why I am cautious about the most popular proposed remedy — simply building more colleges. India has roughly 1.37 lakh MBBS seats across 823 colleges against some 22.7 lakh registered candidates, and the arithmetic obviously demands expansion. But expansion delivered mainly through private colleges is expansion of the inventory that the seat market sells.

And there is a second reason, which matters more. Reservation applies in full in government colleges. In private unaided, minority and deemed institutions its coverage is partial, contested and in places absent. Every seat added privately rather than publicly is a seat added outside the constitutional allocation. Whether India expands publicly or privately is not a financing question. It decides who gets to be a doctor.

Which points at three unglamorous, load-bearing things: public capacity rather than private, measured in supervised clinical placements rather than sanctioned seats; enforceable fee regulation, which the Supreme Court has already directed and which remains among the least implemented of its ten directions; and honest alternative pathways, so that the overwhelming majority who will not get a seat are not treated as failures by a system that never had room for them.

One more thing about scale. The claim that India needs three doctors per thousand people is not a standard — it is near the average of wealthy countries. The commonly cited threshold of one per thousand is itself contested and frequently misattributed. India's official figure of one doctor per 811 people counts AYUSH practitioners; the allopathic figure is closer to 0.7 to 0.8 per thousand. Whether India's problem is absolute shortage or maldistribution changes what "more seats" is even supposed to fix, and it deserves an answer before another decade of college sanctions.

EThe guardrailWhat has to bound all of this

Read the recommendations above together and notice what they build: continuous clinical-activity reporting from teaching hospitals, quota status certified before the exam, every counselling action bound to a verified individual, faculty rosters and volumes published. Each is defensible alone. Together they are a permanent verification layer sitting over 22.7 lakh candidates, their families' income and caste documents, and the patients of every teaching hospital in India.

I am recommending that architecture, so I should say what has to bound it. Without limits, the people least able to object are the same people already carrying every access cost in this system.

  • Patient data is not regulatory telemetry. Clinical-volume regulation runs on aggregate counts computed inside the hospital and reported as numbers. No patient-level record leaves the institution. Teaching-hospital patients are overwhelmingly poor and cannot meaningfully decline care; they must not become the unit of account for a college's recognition.
  • Purpose limitation in law. Admissions and integrity data usable for admissions integrity and nothing else. No linkage to other government databases, no law-enforcement sharing absent a judicial order tied to a specific case.
  • Publication with identifiers stripped. "Publish everything" means faculty by registration number, institution and full-time-equivalent status, cross-checkable against the national register — not personal identifiers of individual doctors.
  • No candidate excluded by an authentication failure. If binding registration to the candidate means biometric or Aadhaar verification, then failure triggers an alternative path — face match, document verification, in-person capture logged for audit — and the candidate proceeds. Verification concerns are resolved afterwards on a full evidence trail, not at the door. False-rejection rates cluster in exactly the households least able to contest them, and a rejection at a counselling gate costs a year.
  • Device assumptions fail for women first. Any confirmation mechanism that assumes the candidate personally controls a phone will fail for candidates in controlling households. Provide a non-device route by default, not on request.
  • Candidate-facing transparency in the candidate's own language. At registration, in all thirteen examination languages: what is collected, how long it is kept, who can see it, and how to contest a flag raised in your name.
  • Hard retention limits and a named accountability path under the data protection law, with an independent grievance route that does not run through the counselling authority.

If this architecture ships without those limits, the argument above will have helped build something I did not intend.

FCounter-argumentWhere this could be wrong

This can be picked up by people I do not want as allies — twice.

The first misuse: "the seat market is the real problem, not the exam" is one short step from "so stop worrying about the exam," and from there to defending the paper-based status quo the leak economy depends on. To be explicit: ship computer-based testing, ship it in 2027, secure it against insiders because an insider is who did it. The claim here is that it is necessary and radically insufficient.

The second misuse is worse. This piece documents fraud in the NRI quota, notes claims of fraud in EWS certification, and criticises a notice that widened a commercial category. Someone will quote that as "the quota system is riddled with fraud." So, explicitly: the categories examined here — NRI, management, institutional — exist to sell seats at ₹1 to ₹4 crore each. Reservation exists to open a profession that was closed by law and custom, for most of Indian history, to most Indians. They are not the same object, and evidence about one is not evidence about the other. On the record I could find, documented EWS fraud in medical admissions is thin and documented reservation fraud thinner still, while the commercial quotas are where the crores actually move. Anyone using this piece against reservation is using it against its argument.

The evidence base is thinner than its confidence suggests. Much of the allocation-market record rests on one state's investigation, one tax department action, and a set of High Court judgments whose texts I could not retrieve and have therefore not relied on. No arrest has been made. If the Telangana case ends without a prosecution complaint, some of what I describe as documented reverts to alleged. The honest position is that India's seat market is better evidenced than most public commentary assumes and worse evidenced than a prosecution would require.

The certainty-over-severity argument has a real limit. Singapore built certainty with a small, wealthy, tightly governed jurisdiction and an investigation bureau reporting to the executive. That does not transplant, and importing enforcement architecture without accountability constraints is how you get a bureau that is certain about the government's enemies. If this argument is used to justify concentrating investigative power rather than shortening trials, it has been misused. Shortening trials is the part with no downside.

And the strongest objection to the whole piece is that the reforms I want are already ordered and simply not implemented — which would suggest the binding constraint is political will, and no amount of better design changes it. I think that is half right, and it is why section D is about price rather than process. If the next two years bring the match, the continuous reporting and the published statistics, and the seat market continues at the same scale, the design argument loses and the political-economy argument wins.
